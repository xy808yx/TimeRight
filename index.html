    The science

    Your eyes do more than see. They help your brain keep time.

    Your daily environment sends light and dark signals. Your eyes pick them up, and your brain uses them to keep your internal timing on track. Time Right turns that biology into a plan.

    Light is the signal

    Bright days and dim nights are your body's strongest timing cue — stronger than any alarm. Your eyes read the gap.

    AH

    Andrew Huberman, Ph.D.

    Neuroscientist · Stanford School of Medicine
    • Morning sunlight within 30–60 min of waking — sets the cortisol pulse and the melatonin timer.
    • Delay caffeine 90–120 min to clear adenosine and avoid the afternoon crash.
    • Train within ~3 hours of waking while cortisol and body temperature rise.
    • Dim lights & cool the room at night; protect the last hours before sleep.

    Your real life

    It doesn't have to be perfect to be timed right.

    01

    Stuck indoors all day

    Offices sit at 200–500 lux; outdoor daylight runs 10,000–25,000+. Short outdoor breaks are the most repeatable fix; a bright lamp is the backup.

    02

    Before-sunrise mornings

    A bright lamp, stepping out as the sky lightens, or sun on the commute all count. Timing matters more than the source.

    03

    Shared home & family schedule

    We protect what you can own: your morning light window and your evening dim-down. The rest stays flexible.

    04

    Inconsistent bedtimes

    A consistent wake time anchors your rhythm even when bedtime drifts. That single habit does more than any other change.
